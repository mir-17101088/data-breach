EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The breaches were found by everyone except the institutions that suffered them

Between 2023 and 2026, 65 data breach incidents affecting both government institutions and the private sector occurred in Bangladesh. Drawing from cybersecurity advisories, dark-web monitoring services, threat-intelligence feeds, and mainstream Bangladeshi newspapers, our findings revealed that of these data breaches, 33 involved government organizations and 32 involved private ones. The data exposed across these incidents includes, but is not limited to: National ID (NID) numbers, biometric records, passport details, and more.

A second finding runs throughout almost every incident in the dataset: the failure of institutions to recognize warning signs of a data breach — institutional blindness. In nearly all documented cases, the breach was identified by external security researchers or dark-web monitoring services, instead of the affected organizations themselves. Where a response can be traced at all, it is far more often silence or denial than an acknowledgment, and a published post-mortem or forensic analysis is rare to the point of near-absence.

"Most organizations in the dataset would have had no way of knowing they had been breached."

On the absence of round-the-clock security operations

Data breaches in Bangladesh have escalated into a critical national security crisis, yet the systemic response remains largely inadequate. Bangladesh still lacks robust accountability mechanisms, mandatory breach notification requirements, and effective institutional responses. This report highlights the immediate risks, identifies critical gaps in existing policy and legal frameworks, and proposes actionable pathways toward institutional accountability.

BACKGROUND

From a laminated card to the key that opens every door

Two decades ago, the average Bangladeshi citizen's life revolved around papers: paying utility bills, accessing government subsidies and welfare, transferring money, or checking a bank balance meant navigating bureaucratic burdens in person. Then the introduction of the national identity (NID) and, in subsequent years, the initiation of Digital Bangladesh and later Smart Bangladesh, significantly changed how day-to-day civic engagements were carried out.

While this digitalisation offers greater convenience to the lives of millions, it has created a fundamental shift in what an identity document does. The paper NID was simply about establishing identity; the smart NID is about access that permeates every walk of life. Each interaction - a tap to check a balance or pay a bill - is a request passed through an API, a small act of permission that lets a system reach into data tied to you: your name, your parents' names, your date of birth, your fingerprints, 32 types of data points in total.

This intermediated access regime isn't new - it existed even in the paper era, just with a human layer wrapped around it. When a citizen walked into a bank, the clerk queried a system that already had programmatic access to the data; the query was simply mediated by a human interface. Digitisation removed that human layer. Over a decade, that accumulation gave Bangladesh an enormous, concentrated store of its citizens' data - and each new service built atop it has extended a further layer of access to government institutions, private bodies, and others. Meanwhile, the safeguards that should accompany such concentration - robust security practice, data-protection law, and institutional oversight -- have consistently been deferred.

DEFINITIONS

What constitutes a data breach?

In plain terms, a data breach is when information about you ends up somewhere it was never supposed to go. The "how" varies: a hacker group breaks into a system and takes it, or an organization simply leaves a digital door open and data spills out by accident. The method and motive differ. What stays constant is the outcome - people's information outside the bounds it was meant to stay within, in hands it was never meant to reach.

Personal Data Protection Act, 2026

"A personal data breach means a breach of security of personal data resulting in unauthorized access to or unlawful transfer, disclosure, alteration, or loss of or intrusion into any personal data processed under this Act, or in the absence of the necessary equipment for proper processing and storage."

In most cases the information at stake is Personally Identifiable Information (PII): any fact, or combination of facts, that can be traced back to a specific person -- a name, a phone number, an NID number, an address, digital payment details, or biometric data such as fingerprints and iris scans. The list is illustrative, not exhaustive.

Why a PII leak is consequential: not all of it can be taken back

Revocable — a lock you can change

A leaked password or payment PIN is recoverable. You reset it and move on.

Irrevocable — yours for life

Your biometrics: the patterns in your fingertips, the structure of your iris -- cannot be reset. Once exposed, they cannot be revoked.

Once a single identity links every essential interaction, the convenience it delivers also carries visibility: the state can see, in one place, what each citizen does. A concentration of data is therefore not only a criminal's prize but also an instrument of surveillance and control. In cybersecurity terms, those who exploit such data are called threat actors — but the same concentrated data a fraudster would steal is also held, lawfully, by the state. Once a single identifier maps identity to activity, it lowers the cost of finding a target, monitoring dissent, and applying pressure at scale.

Of particular concern

The surveillance agency that was itself surveilled

The National Telecommunication Monitoring Centre (NTMC) is mandated to lawfully intercept phone calls, emails, and social media communications in support of criminal investigations by intelligence agencies, to filter and block online content linked to propaganda or criminal activity, and to support broader national security and public order objectives through communication intelligence. According to media sources, its system was exploited using the IDs of two law enforcement officers to collect and sell confidential information — NID details and mobile call data records — for financial gain. A breach at an agency of this nature is categorically different: the data is not merely personal or financial, but intercepted communications and surveillance records that could compromise the safety of individuals being monitored, reveal intelligence methods, or be exploited for blackmail and targeted harassment, or further unauthorized surveillance.

A ministry holding millions of migrant-worker records

The breach reported at the Ministry of Expatriates' Welfare and Overseas Employment reportedly compromised passport records, NID information, eTIN details, and financial and banking documents — identity, tax, and financial information in a single dataset. That combination could be exploited for multiple malicious purposes, including voter manipulation, blackmail, identity-based scams, and financial fraud targeting one of the country's most economically significant demographics.

Custodians of national security and elections

The appearance of armed forces agencies, ministries, and the Bangladesh Election Commission among breached entities raises the stakes considerably. Unlike commercial breaches, where the primary harm is financial or reputational, breaches in this category carry the potential to escalate into national-security incidents or to directly infringe on citizens' constitutional right to privacy.

WHAT THE TIMELINE SHOWS

What follows the breach is the most telling of all: Silence

Without exception, the affected institutions have consistently failed to find the breach, not acknowledging it once others did, and never accounting for how it happened. What recurs across these years is a trend of concealment: breaches brushed over, denied, or left to fade, while the same failures — insecure databases, loose access controls, leaky third-party arrangements, compromised credentials — go unaddressed or are secretly patched because they are never openly named.

Aggregated totals also flatten the picture. A count of incidents tells you how often breaches happened, but not that the same target was hit deliberately, more than once — and it is in the repetition where intent shows itself. Several institutions were struck on separate occasions: the road-transport authority, the armed forces, the national police, and, most tellingly, the country's migrant-worker data systems.

Being breached twice within a span of weeks means either that the first breach taught the institution nothing, or that the attackers never left. When the pharmaceutical company Novo Nordisk was hacked, attackers accessed its code repository via an access token and carried out further intrusions for over two months. And the population exposed in Bangladesh's case is among the most vulnerable to follow-on fraud: migrant workers and their families, who already navigate recruitment agents, visa brokers, and overseas paperwork, are exactly the people a scammer armed with a leaked passport number can most easily deceive — with risks extending to proxy voting, manipulated postal ballots, and targeted voter suppression.

Remittances are predictable and repeated — resilient through the pandemic, rebounding within months of the 2024 upheaval — and that rhythm turns the workers behind them into predictable, repeated marks. A breached passport number is valuable every time the next remittance is due. Attackers were not after a one-off haul; they recognised a standing source of value that pays out with each cycle of money and documents moving between a worker abroad and home.

The bigger takeaway: the data being exposed has grown more permanent and more central to civic life over time, even as the institutions holding it have shown, through repeated breaches, that they are not learning from each hit.

LEGAL ANALYSIS

The accountability deficit in Bangladesh's data-breach response framework

65 documented breach incidents
0instances of legal action - administrative, regulatory, or criminal against a breached entity or responsible party

This absence of enforcement is legally significant not because Bangladesh lacks a statutory framework governing data protection, but because the framework that does exist has not yet produced an operative accountability mechanism capable of translating statutory rights into enforceable outcomes.

Bangladesh's data governance architecture comprises older, sector-specific instruments — such as the National Identity Registration Act, 2023, which criminalises unauthorised access to and disclosure of NID data while preserving formal pathways for third-party access — and a newer, more comprehensive suite of laws enacted in 2026: the Personal Data Protection Act, the National Data Management Act, the Cyber Protection Act, and the Bangladesh Telecommunication (Amendment) Act.

On their face, these instruments establish the constituent elements of an enforcement regime: data-subject rights (ss. 11–13 PDPA), a complaints mechanism before a regulatory Authority (s. 31), administrative penalties of up to BDT 25 lakh or 50 lakh depending on fiduciary classification (s. 32), compensation to affected data subjects (s. 35), and criminal investigation and tribunal-based remedies for cyber-enabled data theft under the Cyber Security Act (ss. 31–35). The absence of a single prosecuted or adjudicated case across 65 documented breaches, despite this apparatus, indicates a substantial gap between statutory design and operative enforcement.

The consequence is a legal accountability vacuum. Statutory obligations exist, and remedial provisions are drafted, but who bears responsibility for a given breach, through which forum liability is to be established, and under what timeline enforcement is to occur remain legally unsettled. Until the transitional provisions are activated, the relationship between regulatory, civil, and criminal remedies is clarified, and the independence of the enforcing Authority from public-sector fiduciaries is secured, Bangladesh's data protection regime is likely to remain a framework of formal rights without a corresponding structure of practical enforcement.

Three interlocking legal deficiencies

1

The remedies are not yet switched on

The PDPA's most consequential remedial provisions — ss. 31–35, covering complaints, penalties, and compensation — are subject to delayed commencement pending a transitional period and separate government notification under s. 1(3). A remedy that has not been activated cannot be invoked: for much of the period under review, the formal legal basis for redress may not have been operative even in principle.

2

Authority-centred, not victim-centred

The Act does not clearly establish an independent civil route through which affected individuals may bring claims before ordinary courts without first proceeding through the regulator, nor how compensation under s. 35 interacts with existing tort, constitutional, or civil-law claims. Ambiguity as to forum and standing is itself a barrier to enforcement, independent of institutional capacity.

3

Who regulates the state when the state is the breacher?

Government offices account for the largest share of reported incidents, yet the framework does not adequately resolve accountability where the breaching entity is itself a public body. Neither the PDPA nor the National Data Management Act, 2026 clearly displaces the possibility that violations by government officials are handled through internal departmental measures. Where the enforcing Authority sits within the same data-management architecture as the agencies it regulates, the entity empowered to establish liability lacks clear independence from the entities most likely to incur it.

SOPS AND WHY THEY MATTER

The law translated into daily discipline — arriving late, unevenly, or not at all

Bangladesh's legal weakness has been compounded by an operational one. Standard operating procedures (SOPs) — foundational for accountability — tend to arrive after a system is already live, unevenly, or not at all. An SOP is essentially the law translated into daily discipline: the rules for vendor onboarding, access control, API governance, data retention, encryption, incident escalation, and breach notification. Without these fundamentals being formalized, even a sound statute remains abstract.

The pattern runs through Bangladesh's largest exposures. The 2023 civil-registration leak came from an insecure public portal, found by an outside researcher rather than internal monitoring; the Smart NID Telegram leak was traced to third parties holding authorised verification access; and the NTMC database sat open for months. Each points to the same missing guardrails: pre-launch security review, partner due diligence, least-privilege access, retention limits, continuous monitoring, and rapid notification. None are exotic; all are what mature systems institutionalise before they scale.

The deeper gap: even where procedures exist on paper, nothing independently confirms they work. Mature digital states close that gap with independent external certification — information-security management certified to ISO/IEC 27001 for the institutions themselves, and SOC 2 assurance reports demanded of the vendors and cloud providers that build and host their systems (the layer where several of Bangladesh's exposures originated). Bangladesh's 2026 laws now reference standards, audits, and breach handling, but they neither mandate recurring, independent verification nor make pre-deployment SOPs a condition of going live.

LOOKING AHEAD

An uneven future requires stronger fundamentals

The arrival of capable general-purpose AI further augments the state's surveillance capacity, but the implications run further — into the offensive and defensive cyber capabilities now advancing quickly at the frontier and creating an uneven playing field.

In April 2026, the American AI lab Anthropic declined to release its most capable model to the public, restricting it instead to a coalition of critical-infrastructure organizations through an initiative it called Project Glasswing, on the grounds that the model could autonomously find and exploit software vulnerabilities at a scale that made open release too dangerous. The company's own stated expectation is that comparable capabilities will emerge at other labs within six to twelve months, potentially without equivalent safeguards.

The same models that let an attacker find vulnerabilities faster also let a defender patch them faster, and the advantage falls to whichever side has access. It is already a live policy dispute: financial regulators in several countries, including the Bank of England, have publicly objected to being locked out of frontier defensive models, while the developers argue that releasing such capability without misuse safeguards would arm attackers as readily as defenders. There is, as yet, no settled answer — and a country still outsourcing its breach discovery to volunteers is in no position to wait for one.

More does not automatically translate to more safety — the contest is more subtle. Attackers have begun turning AI safety mechanisms against the defender: in one case documented by Socket Security and highlighted by Citizen Lab's John Scott-Railton, malware developers inserted text referencing nuclear and biological weapons into their spyware as bait, so that an AI security scanner analysing the code would trigger its own safety refusals and abandon the analysis before reaching the malicious component. As more models ship with aggressive refusals, they carry second-order blind spots that attackers can map and exploit. And in a recent case from India, a job applicant used almost all of the frontier models to find vulnerabilities in a government recruitment portal — then withdrew the applications of his competitors to improve his own chances.

JUNE 2026

While compiling this report, one crashed password-recovery page handed us the keys to a government system

During a routine assessment of a public-facing government portal, TGI's research team tested the site's password-recovery feature. The backend server crashed — and because developers had left debug mode active, the crash printed the portal's entire internal configuration file directly into the browser: plain-text administrative passwords to the master database, the cryptographic keys used to validate every active user session, and login credentials for the government's official email system.

The National Data Center's security systems did block direct external connections to the database — but that protection only matters if an attacker is forcing their way in. These credentials don't require force: they are the authentication itself. Whoever holds them can log in as a legitimate user from inside the network perimeter, and the system has no way to tell the difference.

The findings were reported to BGD e-GOV CIRT the same day. Their response did not dispute anything documented — in fact, it acknowledged identifying further gaps in the system.

Our diagnosis

The threat Bangladesh's digital infrastructure actually faces is not, for the most part, the one that dominates the conversation. Dramatic vectors — AI, foreign influence operations, hostile intelligence services — command a disproportionate share of attention because each is conceptually legible, politically resonant, and carries spectacle. The interventions that would materially reduce exposure — SOC 2 compliance, ISO 27001 certification, rebuilding identity architecture, a bug-bounty program, sustained threat intelligence — are costly, slow, and electorally unrewarding. Attention flows to the threats easiest to dramatise, while the work that closes the actual gaps remains chronically deferred.

The foundational cause is not a sophisticated adversary but a regime of complacency — where so-called "revolutionary" systems are brought into operation before anyone has verified that they are secure. A sophisticated adversary merely exploits the circumstances that complacency enables.

METHODOLOGY & FULL DATASET

How this dossier was built

This report utilizes a descriptive, exploratory mixed-methods design to document and characterize data breach incidents affecting Bangladeshi institutions between January 2023 and May 2026. The unit of analysis is the individual breach incident. Incidents were identified through systematic review of four categories of open sources: localised threat-intelligence advisories from Bangladesh Cyber Security Intelligence (BCSI); international ransomware and dark-web monitoring services (Ransomware.live, Ransomlook.io, RedPacket Security); threat-alert aggregators, principally Daily Dark Web; and reporting in national dailies including The Daily Star, Prothom Alo, and The Business Standard, alongside international outlets such as WIRED and TechCrunch.

For each incident, researchers logged the date of disclosure, the affected organization and its sector, the categories of data exposed, the attack vector where known, the channel of discovery, whether the affected organization publicly acknowledged the incident, and whether any post-mortem or forensic account was published. Duplicate claims referring to the same underlying incident were merged.

Limitations. The dataset reflects only reported and discoverable incidents; the true frequency is almost certainly higher. Volume figures originating from criminal sources are threat-actor claims and may be exaggerated for extortion purposes. Where an organization did not respond or an incident could not be independently corroborated, it is retained in the dataset but tiered accordingly.
